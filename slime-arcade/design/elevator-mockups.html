Shaft

Left quarter of the screen, floor to ceiling. The car runs in it on a visible cable, and a small readout at the top shows the floor it is on and which way it is heading. That is the only arrow in the game — it describes the lift, not a person.

Floors

Bottom 1 · U‑Bahn, middle 2 · S‑Bahn, top 3 · Ausgang und Geschäfte. Each has one colour, used on the floor sign, on people's badges and on the ring around its console button.

Trains

They slide in from the right on floors 1 and 2, stop, open their doors, let one to three people out, and leave again — whether or not the lift ever moves. The station runs on its own; driving it is the game.

People

Four boxes and a badge. They walk to the shaft, queue in order, board on their own when the doors are open, and get out at their number. Tapping one says the number aloud.

Car

Holds three. A fourth person waits for the next trip — patiently, because nobody in this game ever gets impatient.

Console

1 · 2 · 3 left to right, the direction he counts in, each ringed in its floor's colour. Then doors‑open, doors‑close, and the station bell.

Pips

Five little lamps in the top‑right corner; each delivered passenger fills one, the fifth fires the confetti and resets. A reason to keep going with nothing to lose — and the first thing to cut if the screen feels busy.

Why a number beats an arrow

You asked me to think this through. Numbers are the right call, and the reason is stronger than "numbers are nicer" — an arrow would barely say anything in a building this short.

An arrow says almost nothing

With three floors, the top and bottom have exactly one possible direction. Two floors out of three, the arrow tells you something you could already see. A number is a fact on every floor.

Coded three ways at once

A child who can't read a "3" yet can still count the dots, or just match blue to blue. Any one of the three routes gets him to the right button — the game works before numbers do, and teaches them on the way.

It survives the ride

An arrow is spent the moment someone boards. The badge rides along, so when the doors open at 2 you can see at a glance which of your three passengers gets out. That is what makes a full car readable.

One step, not two

Badge says 2, press 2. An arrow would need a second thought — "up from floor 1 means 2" — which turns a matching game into a deduction game. And it quietly rehearses the counting screen he already plays.

And it comes off when it's done

The moment the doors open on the floor a person asked for, their number pops off in a little puff of paper. Nobody on screen with a number is where they want to be — so the remaining work is countable at a glance, and a delivered passenger visibly stops asking for anything.

Where the arrows went

They didn't vanish — they moved to where they're actually true. One up/down indicator sits at the top of the shaft showing which way the car is travelling. That's the lift's own state, and the only place an arrow carries information a number doesn't.

The console, and the rules around it

Five controls. Everything else on screen happens on its own.

1 · 2 · 3

Round, ivory, 54 px, each ringed in its floor's colour so the badge‑to‑button match can be made on colour alone. Pressed, it lights amber and holds that light for the whole trip, then goes out on arrival.

Doors

Arrows away from the leaves opens, arrows toward them closes — the train screen's own glyphs. Doors also open by themselves on arrival and close after four idle seconds, but never while someone is walking through.

Bell

The station gong. No purpose at all, which is the purpose.

Same floor

Press the floor you're already on and the doors simply re‑open. Never an error.

Mid‑ride

A press while the car is moving buzzes softly and is ignored. No hidden queue, nothing pending — what you see is what the lift is doing.

Doors open

The car won't move with its doors open; press a floor and it closes them first, then goes. The order is always visible.

Everything taps

Tap a person and they say their number. Tap a train and it sounds its horn. Nothing on screen ignores a finger.

What it costs to build

Still one file, no build step, no external assets. The DOS feeling comes from a coarse grid, square corners on all the scenery, hard two‑frame walk animation instead of smooth easing, and the whole building being visible at once.

Deliberately left out: a score, a timer, impatient passengers, real lift call logic, and walking the people yourself. The lift is the only thing you drive, which is exactly why one small console is enough.
